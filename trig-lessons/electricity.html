What makes electricity flow?

Before any numbers or formulas: what does electricity need to flow at all? Go through the 7 short steps one at a time. Then switch the circuit on, break it, and try the practice.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    Why does a torch need a button?

    A new battery next to a good bulb gives no light. The torch button doesn't add anything, yet it turns the light on and off. This level finds out why.

    Puzzle 1. Tap the torch in the picture to press its button: light. Tap again: dark. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away. So what did the button do? Watch the metal strip under the button.

    Puzzle 2. Put a brand-new battery on the table, right next to a good bulb, not joined by any wire. The bulb stays dark.

    The battery is full and the bulb works. Something else is needed. By the end of this level, you'll know exactly what.

    Why start here?

    Every later level measures something about flowing electricity: how much flows, how hard it's pushed, what slows it down. None of that makes sense until you know what makes it flow at all.

    The same idea explains why a light switch works, why a broken fairy light can make a whole string go dark, and why electricians start by checking for a "broken loop".

  2. 2
    The big idea

    Electricity flows round a loop, like water in pipes

    A battery pushes electric charges round a loop of wire, like a pump pushes water round a loop of pipe. The charges go round and round. None are used up.

    Picture water in a loop of pipe. A pump pushes the water round and round. On the way it turns a water wheel.

    A circuit works the same way. Each part of the water loop has a partner in the circuit:

    Pump
    →
    Battery بطارية: pushes
    Pipes
    →
    Wires أسلاك: the path
    Water
    →
    Electric charges شحنات: what moves
    Tap
    →
    Switch مفتاح: opens or closes the path
    Water wheel
    →
    Bulb مصباح: uses the flow

    The flow of electric charges is called electric current التيار الكهربي.

    The charges are already in the wire

    A battery doesn't fill the wire with charges. The wire is already full of them, like a pipe already full of water. The battery only pushes them along.

    That's why a light comes on the moment you press the switch. The charges near the bulb start moving straight away. They don't have to travel from the battery first.

    Nothing gets used up

    The water wheel doesn't use up water. The same water comes back to the pump. The bulb doesn't use up charges either. What the bulb uses is the energy the battery gives the charges. You'll meet that energy in Levels 3 and 8.

  3. 3
    What it needs

    Two things are needed for current to flow

    Current needs (1) a source, like a battery, to push the charges, and (2) a closed circuit: a complete loop with no gaps. Switch off = open circuit = no current anywhere.
    • A source, like a battery, to push the charges.
    • A closed circuit دائرة مغلقة: a complete loop with no gaps, so the charges have a path all the way round.

    A switch opens and closes the loop. Switch on: the loop is closed and current flows. Switch off: the loop is open, and current stops.

    One gap stops the current everywhere in the loop, not just after the gap. It's like a water loop: block the pipe in one place and the water stops moving all the way round.

    Why everywhere, and not just after the gap?

    The charges move like a chain of people holding hands in a circle. If one pair lets go, the chain can't be pulled round any more. Everyone stops, even the people far from the break.

    What counts as a gap?

    An open switch, a cut wire, a loose connection, or a broken bulb. Inside a bulb is a very thin wire called the filament. When it snaps, that's a gap in the loop. That's why a "blown" bulb stays dark.

    How your book asks it

    "Give reason: there must be a source in the circuit." To push the charges.
    "Give reason: the circuit must be closed." So the charges flow along a closed path.

  4. 4
    Why charges move

    Charges only move when there's a difference in push

    Water flows from a higher tank to a lower one, and stops when the levels are equal. Charges also need a difference between two points to flow. A battery keeps that difference going. A flat battery has no difference left.

    Two tanks are joined by a pipe with a tap. Tank X is filled higher than tank Y. Open the tap, and water flows from X to Y.

    The flow stops when the two levels are equal. No difference, no flow.

    Electric charges work the same way. They flow only when there's a difference between two points in the circuit. Your book calls it the potential difference فرق الجهد. Level 3 is all about it.

    A battery is like a pump that keeps one tank higher than the other, so the flow never stops. A flat battery is like two tanks that have levelled out: no difference, so no current.

    Why can't the tanks keep flowing on their own?

    As water runs from X to Y, X goes down and Y goes up. The difference shrinks until it's gone. Without a pump to lift the water back, the flow always ends.

    A battery does that lifting job for charges, using the chemicals inside it. When the chemicals are used up, it can't make a difference any more. That's a flat battery.

    How your book says it

    "When the tap is opened, water flows from X to Y, because the water pressure in X is greater." It stops "because the water pressure on both sides of the tap becomes equal."

  5. 5
    Which way?

    Current flows from + to −

    In diagrams, current goes from the battery's + end, round the circuit, to its − end. The electrons that really move go the other way, from − to +. Both describe the same flow.

    A battery has two ends: + (positive) and − (negative). There are two ways to describe which way things move:

    Current (what we draw)

    From + to −, round the outside of the battery. Every book and diagram uses this.

    Electrons (what really moves)

    From − to +. Electrons are tiny charges that move through the wire. They're negative.

    Why two answers? Scientists agreed on "+ to −" in the 1700s, long before anyone knew about electrons. Later, they found the moving charges were negative and go the other way. By then the rule was everywhere, so it stayed.

    Does it matter for a bulb?

    No. The bulb lights the same either way. The direction only matters when you draw diagrams and read meters, and there we always use + to −.

    How your book says it

    "Electric current flows from the point of higher electric potential (the positive pole) to the point of lower potential (the negative pole), according to scientific convention." That's the water idea again: from the higher tank to the lower one.

  6. 6
    Where it comes from

    Two kinds of supply, and four things to measure

    Direct current (DC) flows one way, steadily: batteries. Alternating current (AC) keeps changing direction: generators and the socket at home. Next levels measure current, potential difference, resistance and energy.

    Direct current (DC)

    Flows in one direction, at a steady strength.

    From: a cell, a battery, a car battery, a power bank.

    Alternating current (AC)

    Keeps changing direction, back and forth, many times a second.

    From: a generator, and the sockets in your home.

    To understand a circuit, we measure four things. Each one gets its own level:

    • Level 2CurrentHow much charge flows each second.
    • Level 3Potential differenceHow hard the charges are pushed.
    • Level 4ResistanceWhat slows the flow down.
    • Level 8Energy and powerWhat the flow can do, and what it costs.

    How fast does AC change?

    In Egypt, the mains supply changes direction 100 times every second (50 full back-and-forth cycles). It's far too fast to see, so a lamp on AC looks steady.

    Why do phones need a charger?

    Phone batteries need DC, but the socket gives AC. Part of the charger's job is to turn AC into DC.

    The same four in water

    Current is how much water flows past each second. Potential difference is how much higher one tank is than the other. Resistance is how narrow the pipe is. Energy is the work the water does, like turning the wheel.

  7. 7
    Back to the torch

    Now we can solve step 1

    The torch button is a switch: it closes the loop (light) or opens it (dark). A battery on the table next to a bulb has no closed loop, so no current flows.
    • The button is a switch. Pressing it closes the loop, so charges flow and the bulb lights. Pressing again opens the loop, and everything stops.
    • The battery on the table has a push, but no wire joins it to the bulb, so there's no closed path from + back to −. No loop, no current.

    If a torch doesn't work, check the two things from step 3:

    • Is the loop closed? A loose spring, a dirty contact or a broken bulb filament all leave a gap.
    • Is there a push? A flat battery has no difference left between its ends.

    • Electricians

      The first test for a dead light is "is the loop broken somewhere?"

    • Circuit breakers

      In your home, they open the loop on purpose when something is dangerous.

    • Car makers

      The car body is part of the loop, so only one wire is needed to each light.

    A string of fairy lights

    In some old strings, all the bulbs are in one single loop. When one bulb breaks, it makes a gap, and the whole string goes dark, even though the other bulbs are fine. Level 7 shows how homes are wired so that one broken lamp doesn't switch off everything else.

    Why is a switch safe to touch?

    Its moving parts are inside a plastic case. Plastic doesn't let charges flow, so your finger isn't part of the loop. Level 4 explains why some materials let current through and others don't.
