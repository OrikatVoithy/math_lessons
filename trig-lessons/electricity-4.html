What slows the flow? (Resistance)

Current is the flow and potential difference is the push. Now: what gets in the way? Go through the 7 short steps, then design your own wire in the lab and try the practice.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    Why does the filament glow, but not the wires?

    In an old-style light bulb, the same current flows through the thin filament and the thick wires holding it. Yet the filament glows white-hot and the wires stay cool. The difference is resistance.

    Look inside an old-style light bulb. A very thin, coiled wire (the filament) is held up by two thicker wires.

    They're all part of the same loop, so the same current flows through all of them. Yet the filament glows white-hot, while the thick wires stay much cooler.

    What's different about the filament? By the end of this level, you'll be able to explain it in your book's words.

    Same current, very different heat

    Charges pass through every part of a single loop at the same rate. So the glow isn't about how much current flows. It's about how hard the charges find it to get through each part.

    Why it matters

    The same idea explains kettles, hair dryers, toasters and electric heaters (all made to get hot), and why cables are made thick enough not to get hot.

  2. 2
    The big idea

    Resistance is what gets in the way of the charges

    Electric resistance is the opposition charges meet as they move through a conductor, like road humps slowing cars. It's measured in ohms (Ω) with an ohmmeter.

    Road humps slow cars down. In the same way, every conductor has something that slows down the charges moving through it.

    Your book's definition

    Electric resistance (R) المقاومة الكهربية: the opposition encountered by electric charges as they move through conductors.

    It's measured in ohms (Ω) أوم. Ω is the Greek letter omega.

    To measure it, connect an ohmmeter الأوميتر to the two ends (terminals) of the resistor.

    Why do charges meet opposition?

    A wire is made of particles (atoms). As the free electrons move through, they keep bumping into these particles, like people hurrying through a crowded hall. Every bump slows them down. That's resistance.

    Who was Ohm?

    Georg Simon Ohm, a German physicist, studied how current depends on voltage and resistance in the 1820s. You'll meet his law in Level 5.

    Water idea

    A long, narrow, rough pipe resists water more than a short, wide, smooth one. Wires work the same way, as the next step shows.

  3. 3
    What it depends on

    Three things decide a wire's resistance

    Longer wire → more resistance. Thicker wire (bigger cross-section) → less resistance. A material with higher resistivity → more resistance.

    At a given temperature, every wire's resistance depends on three things:

    FactorRelationshipWhich meansPicture it
    1. LengthDirectly proportionalLonger wire → more resistanceA longer corridor full of people: more bumps on the way
    2. Cross-sectional area (thickness)Inversely proportionalThicker wire → less resistanceA wider door: more people get through at once
    3. Resistivity of the materialDirectly proportionalHigher resistivity → more resistanceSome halls are more crowded than others
    Your book's definition

    Resistivity المقاومة النوعية: a characteristic physical property of a material that expresses how much it resists the passage of electric current through it.

    Cross-sectional area?

    Cut the wire straight across and look at the cut end: that circle is the cross-section. Its area (in mm²) tells you how thick the wire is.

    "Characteristic" means it belongs to the material

    Every piece of copper has the same resistivity, whatever its shape. That's why resistivity helps tell materials apart, just like density does.

    How your book asks it

    "Mention the factors that increase the resistance of a conductor at a given temperature." Increasing its length; decreasing its cross-sectional area (thickness); increasing the resistivity of its material.

  4. 4
    Comparing wires

    Which wire has more resistance?

    Same material: compare length (longer = more) and thickness (thinner = more). If both changes push the same way, the answer is clear. A quick trick: compare length ÷ area.

    Your book often gives two or more wires of the same material and asks which resists more. Check both factors:

    Example from your book

    A wire 8 m long with a cross-section of 8 mm² is replaced by one of the same material, 12 m long and 2 mm². What happens to the resistance?

    1. Longer (8 → 12 m): resistance goes up.
    2. Thinner (8 → 2 mm²): resistance goes up.
    3. Both push the same way: the resistance increases.
    A quick trick

    For the same material, work out length ÷ area for each wire. The bigger number has the bigger resistance.

    1. Old wire: 8 ÷ 8 = 1.
    2. New wire: 12 ÷ 2 = 6.
    3. 6 is bigger, so the new wire resists more (about 6 times as much).

    When the factors push opposite ways

    A wire that is twice as long and twice as thick has the same resistance as the original: twice the length doubles it, twice the thickness halves it. Length ÷ area: 2 ÷ 2 = 1, no change. That's why the trick is useful.

    Only for the same material

    If the materials are different, resistivity matters too, and you'd need to know how they compare (next step).

  5. 5
    Materials and heat

    The material, and the temperature, matter too

    Copper has a low resistivity, so it's used for wires. Tungsten's is higher. Plastic barely lets current through at all. Heating a wire makes its particles vibrate faster, so the charges bump into them more and the resistance goes up.

    Some materials let current through easily. Others resist it strongly. Here's roughly how much more some metals resist than copper, for wires of the same size:

    • Copper
    • Aluminium
    • Tungsten
    • Iron
    • Nichrome

    Copper is used for wires because it resists least. Nichrome is used in heaters because it resists a lot. Plastic and rubber resist so strongly that almost no current passes, so they cover wires to keep us safe.

    Temperature: as a wire gets hotter, its particles vibrate faster. The moving charges bump into them more often, so the resistance increases.

    The numbers behind the bars

    For wires of the same size, aluminium resists about 1.6 times as much as copper, tungsten about 3.3 times, iron about 6 times, and nichrome about 65 times.

    Why aren't all wires copper, then?

    Copper is expensive and heavy. The huge overhead power lines use aluminium, which is lighter and cheaper, and they make up for its higher resistivity by being thicker.

    How your book says it

    "As the temperature of the conductor increases, the vibration speed of its particles increases; consequently, the number of collisions with electric charges and with one another increases, hindering the movement of free electrons and increasing the conductor's resistance."

  6. 6
    Changing resistance on purpose

    The rheostat: a resistor you can adjust

    A sliding rheostat is a variable resistor. Moving its slider changes how much wire the current goes through: more wire, more resistance, less current, dimmer lamp. A radio's volume knob works the same way.

    A sliding rheostat الريوستات is a long coil of wire with a slider. The current only goes through the part of the coil between the end and the slider.

    Move the slider to put more wire in the circuit: the resistance goes up, the current goes down and the lamp gets dimmer. Less wire: the lamp gets brighter. Try it with the slider below the picture.

    Life application: turning a radio's volume knob changes a resistance, which changes the current, which changes how loud it is.

    The scientific basis, in your book's words

    "By increasing the length of wire inserted into the circuit by the slider, the wire's electric resistance increases, so the circuit's total resistance increases and the current intensity flowing through it decreases."

    A typical exam question

    "The slider is moved so less of the rheostat's wire is in the circuit. What happens to the ammeter reading and the lamp's brightness?" Both increase: less wire means less resistance, so more current flows, and the lamp is brighter.

  7. 7
    Back to the light bulb

    Now we can solve step 1

    The filament is thin, long and made of tungsten (higher resistivity), so its resistance is high. High resistance turns part of the electrical energy into heat as well as light, so it glows. The connecting wires are thick, short copper with low resistance, so they stay cool.
    • The filament is thin, long (coiled up to fit) and made of tungsten, which has a high resistivity. All three factors make its resistance high.
    • The connecting wires are thick, short and made of copper, which has a low resistivity, so their resistance is low.
    • Give reason: the filament glows when current passes. Because its high resistance converts part of the electrical energy into heat energy, as well as light energy.

    The rule: for the same current, the higher the resistance, the more heat is made in that part.

    • Heaters and kettles

      Use high-resistance nichrome coils to make heat on purpose.

    • Cable makers

      Use thick copper so cables stay cool and safe.

    • LED lamps

      Replaced filament bulbs, which wasted most of their energy as heat.

    Why tungsten?

    A filament has to glow at over 2500 °C without melting. Tungsten melts at about 3400 °C, higher than almost any other metal.

    Thomas Edison

    An American inventor who developed a long-lasting incandescent light bulb, and also the phonograph (sound recorder) and the movie camera. He held 1093 patents in the United States.

    Why do bulbs usually blow when switched on?

    A cold filament has a lower resistance (step 5), so a big rush of current flows at the moment you switch on. That rush is when a worn, thin spot is most likely to snap.
