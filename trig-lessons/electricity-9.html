Electricity makes magnetism (the electromagnet)

A current in a wire turns it into a magnet, one you can switch on and off. Go through the 7 short steps, then build your own electromagnet and see how many paper clips it lifts.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    How does a crane pick up a car, then let it go?

    In a scrapyard, a crane's giant magnet lifts a whole car and drops it exactly where it's wanted. An ordinary magnet can't let go. This one is made with electricity, so it can be switched off.

    In a scrapyard, a crane lowers a huge disc onto an old car. The car jumps up and sticks to it. The crane swings round and, at exactly the right spot, the car drops.

    An ordinary magnet can't do that: once it grabs something, it holds on. So what kind of magnet can be switched off?

    Where else

    Door bells, loudspeakers, electric locks, MRI scanners in hospitals and the motors in Level 10 all use magnets made with electricity.

  2. 2
    What you already know

    Magnets, fields and the compass

    A magnetic field is the region around a magnet where its force can be detected. Field lines are imaginary lines showing its direction and strength. A compass needle is a small free magnet that points north.
    Magnetic field المجال المغناطيسي
    The region around a magnet where the effects of its magnetic force can be detected.
    Field lines
    Imaginary lines that show the direction and strength of the magnetic field. Where they're close together, the field is strong.
    Compass needle البوصلة
    A small, freely moving magnet that points north because of the Earth's magnetic field.

    Why does a compass point north?

    The Earth itself acts like a giant magnet. A compass needle lines up with the Earth's field lines, so it points north–south.

  3. 3
    Oersted's discovery

    A current makes a magnetic field

    Put a wire above a compass, parallel to its needle. Close the circuit: the needle swings. So an electric current has a magnetic effect: it makes a magnetic field around the wire.

    The book's activity (try it with the picture):

    1. Put a compass on a table and wait for its needle to settle.
    2. Hold an insulated wire just above it, parallel to the needle, with the circuit open.
    3. Close the circuit. What do you see?

    Observation: the needle swings away from its first direction. Conclusion: an electric current has a magnetic effect.

    Give reason: the compass needle near a current-carrying wire deflects. Because the current in the wire makes a magnetic field around it.

    Who was Oersted?

    Hans Christian Oersted, a Danish scientist, noticed this in 1820, during a lecture. It was the first proof that electricity and magnetism are linked. Your book asks: "The scientist … discovered the magnetic effect of electric current." Oersted.

    Switch it off

    Open the circuit and the needle swings back to north: no current, no extra field.

  4. 4
    The electromagnet

    A coil around soft iron makes a magnet you can switch off

    An electromagnet is a soft-iron core wound with insulated copper wire connected to a battery. It's a temporary magnet: switch the current off and it loses its magnetism. Soft iron is used because it's easily magnetised and demagnetised.
    Your book's definition

    Electromagnet المغناطيس الكهربي: a soft-iron core wound with an insulated copper wire connected to a battery. It is a temporary magnet that can be switched on or off.

    • Give reason: the electromagnet is called a temporary magnet. Because it loses its magnetisation when the current in its coil is switched off.
    • Give reason: its core is made of soft iron. Because soft iron is easily magnetised and demagnetised.
    • What happens when the current is switched off? The electromagnet loses its magnetism, and the iron filings or clips it held fall off.

    Why a coil, not a straight wire?

    A straight wire's field is weak. Winding the wire into many turns adds all their fields together, and the iron core concentrates them, making a strong magnet.

    Why insulated wire?

    So the current goes round every turn, instead of jumping straight across where the turns touch.

  5. 5
    Making it stronger

    More turns, more current, a soft-iron core

    An electromagnet's strength is directly proportional to the number of turns in its coil and to the current through it, and depends on the core material (soft iron). In the book's activity, more turns and more cells lifted more paper clips.
    FactorRelationshipWhich means
    Number of turns (n)Directly proportionalMore turns → stronger magnet
    Current (I)Directly proportionalMore current (more cells in series) → stronger magnet
    Core materialDepends on itA soft-iron core makes it much stronger than air

    The book's activity: wind a few turns of insulated wire round a soft-iron nail and connect a cell. Count the paper clips it lifts. Then add more turns, then more cells in series.

    Observation: more turns lifted more clips, and more cells lifted more still. Conclusion: the more turns and the more current, the stronger the electromagnet.

    Why do more cells mean more current?

    Cells in series add up their emf (Level 6), and a bigger push drives a bigger current through the coil (Ohm's law, Level 5).

  6. 6
    Using electromagnets

    The scrap crane and the electric bell

    Scrap crane: switch on to grab iron, switch off to drop it. Electric bell: pressing the button magnetises the electromagnet, it pulls the iron strip so the hammer hits the gong, which opens the circuit, so the strip springs back and closes it again, over and over.
    Book example: moving scrap iron

    When the electromagnet's coil is connected, pieces of iron scrap are attracted to it and can be moved. When the current is switched off, it loses its magnetism and the scrap falls at the place wanted.

    The electric bell (step through it with the buttons under the picture):

    1. Press the button: the circuit closes, so the electromagnet is magnetised and pulls the soft-iron strip.
    2. The strip swings over and the hammer strikes the gong. At the same moment the strip leaves the contact screw, so the circuit opens and the electromagnet loses its magnetism.
    3. The spring pulls the strip back to the contact screw, closing the circuit again. This repeats very fast, so the bell keeps ringing.

    The bell's parts (book)

    A U-shaped electromagnet; a springy soft-iron strip with a hammer at one end; a metal gong; a contact screw that touches the strip when the circuit is closed; a spring; a push button and a source.

  7. 7
    Back to the crane

    Now we can solve step 1

    The crane's disc is an electromagnet. Current on: it's a strong magnet and grabs the car. Current off: it stops being a magnet (soft iron demagnetises easily), so the car drops. A permanent magnet can't be switched off or adjusted.
    • The crane's disc is a giant electromagnet: a coil of wire with a soft-iron core.
    • Current on: a strong magnet that grabs the car. Current off: the soft iron loses its magnetism at once, so the car drops.
    • More current makes it stronger, so the operator can choose how hard it pulls.
    Permanent magnetElectromagnet
    AlikeBoth have a north and a south pole, make a magnetic field, and attract magnetic materials.
    LastsKeeps its magnetism permanentlyLoses it when the current is cut off (temporary)
    StrengthCan't be controlledEasily controlled by the current or the number of turns
