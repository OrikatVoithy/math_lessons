Series and parallel circuits (joining resistors)

Real circuits have many parts. Join them in a line or in branches, and the resistance, the current and the voltage all behave differently. Go through the 7 short steps, then wire up bulbs in the lab and break one.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    One lamp in your home breaks, and the others stay on. Why?

    When one lamp in your home blows, the rest keep working. In an old string of fairy lights, one broken bulb makes them all go dark. The difference is how the parts are joined: in series or in parallel.

    When a lamp in your bedroom blows, the kitchen light, the fridge and the TV all keep working.

    But in some old strings of fairy lights, one broken bulb makes the whole string go dark, as you saw in Level 1.

    Both are circuits with many parts. What's different about how they're joined?

    Why it matters

    How parts are joined decides how bright each lamp is, how much current the battery gives, and what happens when something breaks. Electricians choose the joining on purpose.

  2. 2
    Two ways to join

    In series: one path. In parallel: branches.

    Several resistors can be replaced by one equivalent resistance (R′) with the same effect. Series: the current goes through all of them along one continuous path. Parallel: the current splits into branching paths.

    Practical circuits use several resistors. Their combined effect is called the equivalent resistance.

    Your book's definition

    Equivalent resistance (R′) المقاومة المكافئة: the single resistance that produces the same effect on the properties of an electric circuit as a group of interconnected resistors.

    In series على التوالي

    The current flows through the resistors along one continuous path, one after another.

    In parallel على التوازي

    The current flows through the resistors along branching paths, splitting and joining again.

    Your book compares the two in five ways: how they're joined, the equivalent resistance, the purpose, the current, and the potential difference. The next steps take them one at a time.

    Water idea

    Series is like one pipe with several narrow parts in a row. Parallel is like a pipe that splits into several side-by-side pipes, then joins up again.

  3. 3
    Equivalent resistance

    Series makes a bigger resistance. Parallel makes a smaller one.

    For n identical resistors R: series R′ = R × n (bigger, like one longer wire); parallel R′ = R ÷ n (smaller, like one thicker wire).
    Series: R′ = R × n
    Parallel: R′ = R ÷ n

    Why? Think back to Level 4's three factors:

    Series: one longer resistor

    Resistors in a row act like one longer resistor. Longer means more resistance, so R′ is larger than any one of them.

    Purpose: to get a large equivalent resistance.

    Parallel: one thicker resistor

    Resistors side by side act like one thicker resistor (a bigger cross-section). Thicker means less resistance, so R′ is smaller than any one of them.

    Purpose: to get a small equivalent resistance.

    Book example

    Three identical 6 Ω resistors. Find the equivalent resistance in series and in parallel.

    1. Series: R′ = 6 × 3 = 18 Ω.
    2. Parallel: R′ = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 Ω.

    Different resistors?

    In series, you can always just add them: R′ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃. Your book only asks about identical resistors in parallel, where R′ = R ÷ n.

  4. 4
    The current

    Series: the same current. Parallel: the current splits.

    Total current = V ÷ R′. Series: the same current flows through every resistor. Parallel: the total current is shared equally among identical resistors (each gets total ÷ n), and the branch currents add up to the total.

    First find the total current with Ohm's law, using the equivalent resistance: I = V ÷ R′.

    In series

    There's only one path, so the same current flows through every resistor: I = I₁ = I₂ = I₃.

    In parallel

    The total current is shared among the branches: I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃. With identical resistors, each gets total ÷ n.

    Series example

    Three identical 60 Ω resistors in series with a 45 V source. Find the current through each.

    1. R′ = 60 × 3 = 180 Ω.
    2. I = 45 ÷ 180 = 0.25 A, the same through each.
    Parallel example

    Three identical 30 Ω resistors in parallel with a 45 V source. Find the total current and the current through each.

    1. R′ = 30 ÷ 3 = 10 Ω.
    2. I = 45 ÷ 10 = 4.5 A in total.
    3. Each: 4.5 ÷ 3 = 1.5 A.

    Why does parallel draw more total current?

    Each new branch is an extra path for the charges, so the equivalent resistance falls and the battery pushes more current in total. Like opening more checkout lanes in a shop: more people get through each minute.

  5. 5
    The potential difference

    Series: the voltage is shared. Parallel: each gets the full voltage.

    Series: the source's emf is shared equally among identical resistors (each gets emf ÷ n), and the voltages add up to the emf. Parallel: every resistor gets the full emf of the source.

    In series

    The source's emf is shared among the resistors: V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃. With identical resistors, each gets emf ÷ n.

    In parallel

    Every resistor is connected straight across the source, so each gets the full emf: V = V₁ = V₂ = V₃.

    Book example

    A battery of emf 12 V is connected to two identical 3 Ω resistors. Find the potential difference across each resistor (1) in series, (2) in parallel.

    1. Series: 12 ÷ 2 = 6 V each.
    2. Parallel: 12 V each, the full emf.

    Why are bulbs in series dimmer?

    Each bulb gets only part of the voltage, so less current is pushed through it. Two identical bulbs in series each get half the voltage and half the current: each one is much dimmer than a single bulb on its own.

    Using what you know from Level 6

    We take the battery's internal resistance as negligible, so the voltage across the resistors equals the emf.

  6. 6
    When something breaks

    Series: everything stops. Parallel: only that branch stops.

    Series: if one part breaks, the only path is broken, so the current stops everywhere. Parallel: only the broken branch stops; the others still have their own paths. That's why homes are wired in parallel.

    In series

    If one component is damaged, the current stops in the whole circuit and every part stops working. G.R.: because the only path of the current is broken.

    In parallel

    If one component is damaged, current stops only through that component; the rest keep working. G.R.: because the current flows through several separate paths.

    Life application: household appliances are connected in parallel. G.R.: so that each appliance can be operated on its own, and if one is switched off or damaged, the others keep working.

  7. 7
    Back to your home

    Now we can solve step 1, and meet the pacemaker

    Homes are wired in parallel, so each lamp has its own path and gets the full 220 V. Old fairy lights were in series, so one break stopped them all. The pacemaker uses a tiny battery and microcircuits to send regular electrical pulses to the heart.
    • Your home is wired in parallel: every lamp and appliance has its own branch, gets the full 220 V, and works on its own. One blown lamp only breaks its own branch.
    • Old fairy lights were in series: one path through every bulb. One broken bulb breaks the only path, so all go dark.
    Integration with medicine

    The pacemaker

    A practical application that joins electric circuits with the human body. It's used by people whose heartbeat is irregular.

    Location: implanted under the skin, near the shoulder. Structure: a battery; a smart microchip with microcircuits that manage the device; and wires that connect the microcircuit directly to the heart muscle.

    How it works: when the natural heartbeat becomes irregular, the microchip sends small electrical pulses at regular intervals to the heart, stimulating the heart muscle to contract regularly.

    Christmas lights today

    Modern light strings use cleverer wiring, so one failed bulb usually doesn't switch off the rest, but the old series problem is why they changed.
