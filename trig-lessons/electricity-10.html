The electric motor: from current to motion

Fans, drills, washing machines and electric cars all turn electricity into spinning. Go through the 7 short steps, then run your own motor in the lab.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    How does electricity make a fan spin?

    Plug in a fan and its blades spin. Electricity goes in and motion comes out, with nothing burning and no engine. The part that does it is an electric motor, and it works with a magnet and a coil.

    Switch on a fan, and the blades start spinning. A drill, a hair dryer, a washing machine and an electric car do the same: electricity goes in, turning comes out.

    There's no fuel and no engine. So how can electricity push something round?

    In Level 9, a current made a magnetic field. Today: what happens when a current flows in a wire that's already inside a magnet?

    Motors everywhere

    A typical home has dozens of motors: in the fridge, the fans, the washing machine, the blender, even in a phone's vibration buzzer.

  2. 2
    The key experiment

    A magnet pushes a wire that carries a current

    A metal rod rests on two copper rails between the poles of a strong magnet. Close the circuit: current flows through the rod and it rolls along. A current-carrying wire across a magnetic field feels a magnetic force.

    The book's activity: a metal rod rests across two copper rails, inside a strong U-shaped magnet. The rails are connected to a battery and a switch. Close the switch (try it with the picture):

    Observation: the rod rolls along the rails. Conclusion: when a current flows through a conductor placed across (perpendicular to) magnetic field lines, the conductor feels a magnetic force in a definite direction, which can make it move.

    Reverse the current, and the rod rolls the other way.

    Why does it happen?

    The current makes its own magnetic field around the rod (Level 9). That field pushes against the magnet's field, so the rod is shoved sideways.

  3. 3
    Turning forces

    Two opposite pushes make something turn: torque

    Torque is the turning effect of two equal forces in opposite directions acting along different lines. Like your two hands on a steering wheel: one pushes up, the other down, and the wheel turns.
    Your book's definition

    Torque عزم الازدواج: the rotational effect produced by two forces that are equal in size, opposite in direction, and act along different lines.

    Think of a steering wheel. One hand pushes up on one side, the other pushes down on the other side. The forces are equal and opposite, but they act on different lines, so the wheel turns instead of moving.

    In a motor, the two sides of a coil carry current in opposite directions, so the magnet pushes one side up and the other side down. That's a torque, so the coil turns.

    Why not equal and opposite on the same line?

    Push a box equally from both sides along the same line and nothing happens: the forces cancel. Only when the lines are apart do they make it turn.

  4. 4
    Inside a motor

    Coil, magnet, commutator and brushes

    An electric motor turns electrical energy into kinetic energy. Its parts: a coil of wire that can spin, a permanent magnet around it, a commutator (a split metal cylinder joined to the coil's ends) and two graphite brushes that carry the current in and out.
    Your book's definition

    Electric motor المحرك الكهربي: a device that converts electrical energy into kinetic energy.

    PartWhat it is and does
    CoilA rectangular coil of insulated wire that can spin about its axis.
    Permanent magnetIts N and S poles make the magnetic field around the coil.
    CommutatorA metal cylinder split into two halves insulated from each other. The halves are joined to the coil's ends and spin with the coil.
    Two graphite brushesThey press on the two halves of the commutator. G.R.: to act as the input and output of the electric current.

    Give reason: the electric motor is called by that name. Because it converts electrical energy into kinetic (movement) energy.

    Why graphite?

    Graphite conducts electricity and is slippery, so it can rub against the spinning commutator without wearing it out quickly. Your book asks: "The two motor brushes are made of …" graphite (Monufia 26).

  5. 5
    How it spins

    Current in, torque on the coil, round it goes

    Current enters through a brush and flows round the coil. Its two sides carry current in opposite directions, so the magnet pushes them in opposite directions: a torque. The coil spins. The commutator swaps the current every half turn, so it keeps spinning the same way.
    1. The current enters through one brush and the commutator, flows round the coil, and leaves through the other brush.
    2. The coil sits in the magnet's field, and its two sides carry current in opposite directions.
    3. So the magnet pushes one side up and the other side down: two equal, opposite forces on different lines, a torque.
    4. The torque makes the coil turn.

    Your book's principle: when a current passes through the coil, a torque is produced, causing the coil to rotate.

    Why doesn't it just rock back and forth?

    After half a turn, each side of the coil has swapped places. The commutator's two halves swap brushes at that moment, so the current in the coil reverses. That keeps the push in the same turning direction, half turn after half turn.

  6. 6
    Controlling it

    More power, the other way, faster or slower

    A motor's power grows with its torque, which increases with more current, more turns, a bigger coil face area and a stronger magnet. To reverse it, reverse the current or reverse the magnet's poles.

    A motor's power is directly proportional to the torque on its coil. The torque increases by increasing:

    1The current through the coil (I)
    2The number of turns in the coil (n)
    3The face area of the coil (A)
    4The strength of the magnetic field (B)

    Two ways to reverse a motor (book):

    • Reverse the poles of the electric source connected to the coil.
    • Reverse the poles of the permanent magnet.

    The electric drill (book):

    • For hard walls, the motor turns at a very high speed. G.R.: to give the drill bit enough kinetic energy to cut into the hard surface.
    • Used as a screwdriver, it turns at a low speed. G.R.: for accuracy, and to avoid damaging the screw head while tightening or loosening it.

    Reverse both?

    Reverse the current and the magnet together, and the two changes cancel out: the motor turns the same way as before.

  7. 7
    Back to the fan

    Now we can solve step 1

    A fan has a motor: current flows round a coil inside a magnet, the magnet pushes the coil's two sides in opposite directions, the torque spins the coil, and the blades are fixed to it. Electrical energy becomes kinetic energy.
    • The fan's motor has a coil inside a magnet. Current flows through the brushes and the commutator into the coil.
    • The magnet pushes the coil's two sides in opposite directions, making a torque, so the coil spins, and the blades fixed to it spin too.
    • Energy: electrical energy in, kinetic (movement) energy out.
    • The speed control gives more current for a faster spin, or less for slower.
