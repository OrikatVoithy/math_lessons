Trigonometry · Level 1 of 6

What are sin, cos and tan?

Start with the five short steps below. They explain why these three words exist, what they mean, and how to work out an answer with them. After that, the triangle lab lets you try it yourself.

  1. 1
    Why

    Some things are too hard to measure

    Say you want to know how tall a tree is. You can't climb it with a tape measure. You can't stretch a tape across a river, or up the side of a tall building, either.

    But two things are easy to measure from the ground: how far away you are, and the angle you tilt your head to see the top. Trigonometry turns those two easy numbers into the hard one.

  2. 2
    The big idea

    Same angle means same shape

    A right triangle is a triangle with one square corner (90°), like the corner of a page. If you draw a bigger right triangle with the same angle at the bottom, you get the same shape, only zoomed in.

    Because the shape is the same, the sides keep the same proportions. In the picture, the tall side is always 0.6 times the long sloping side, whether the triangle is small or big.

    So every angle comes with its own fixed set of numbers. Those numbers are sin, cos and tan.

  3. 3
    The names

    First name the sides, then sin, cos and tan

    Pick the angle you care about. Mathematicians call it θ (the Greek letter "theta"). Each side gets a name from where it sits compared with θ:

    Hypotenuse
    The longest side. It is across from the square corner.
    Opposite
    The side across from θ. It doesn't touch θ.
    Adjacent
    The side next to θ that isn't the hypotenuse. "Adjacent" means "next to".

    sin, cos and tan are names for three of the fixed proportions from step 2:

    SOH

    sin = opposite ÷ hypotenuse

    How tall the triangle is compared with its longest side.

    CAH

    cos = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse

    How wide it is compared with its longest side.

    TOA

    tan = opposite ÷ adjacent

    How steep it is. This is the slope.

    Say "soh-cah-toa" out loud to remember it. You never have to work these numbers out yourself: a calculator already knows them for every angle. Type sin 30 and it answers 0.5.

  4. 4
    How to calculate

    A three-step recipe

    1. Write down what you know and what you want. Usually that's one angle, one side you know, and one side you want.
    2. Pick the one that uses those two sides. Check SOH CAH TOA to see which of sin, cos or tan uses both.
    3. Put in the numbers and solve. Get the sin, cos or tan of the angle from the calculator, then multiply or divide.
    Example

    A 5 m ladder leans against a wall. It makes a 60° angle with the ground. How high up the wall does it reach?

    1. Know: the angle is 60°, the ladder is the hypotenuse = 5 m. Want: the height, which is opposite the angle.
    2. Opposite and hypotenuse means SOH, so use sin.
    3. sin 60° = height ÷ 5, so height = 5 × sin 60° = 5 × 0.87 ≈ 4.3 m.
  5. 5
    Where it's used

    Who uses this in real life

    Back to the tree from step 1: you stand 10 m away and look up at 40°. The height is opposite, the 10 m is adjacent, so use tan: height = 10 × tan 40° ≈ 10 × 0.84 ≈ 8.4 m. No climbing needed.

    • Builders

      Work out how steep a roof or a wheelchair ramp is, and how long the boards must be.

    • Video games

      When a character runs at an angle, cos tells the game how far to move across and sin how far to move up.

    • Maps and GPS

      Surveyors and phones find positions and distances from angles to known points.

    • Pilots and sailors

      Work out the real direction to steer when wind or current pushes them sideways.

    • Engineers

      Split the pull on a bridge cable into the part that holds it up and the part that pulls sideways.

    • Astronomers

      Measured the distance to the Moon and to nearby stars using angles, long before rockets.
