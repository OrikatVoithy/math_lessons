Heights and distances

You know how to find sides and angles. Now use them to measure real things you can't reach: tall buildings, boats out at sea, planes in the sky. Go through the 7 short steps one at a time.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    Why learn this?

    Measure things you can't reach

    How tall is that building? How far away is that boat? You can't hold a tape measure up a skyscraper, or stretch one across the sea.

    But you can stand somewhere safe, measure one distance, and measure one angle. Trigonometry does the rest.

    • Mount Everest was first measured this way, in the 1850s. Surveyors measured angles to the top from over 100 km away, and worked out its height.
    • Sailors work out how far they are from a lighthouse, because its height is printed on their map.
    • Pilots use the angle down to the runway to know how far away it is.
  2. 2
    Looking up

    The angle of elevation

    When you look up at something, the angle of elevation is the angle between:

    • a flat (horizontal) line straight ahead from your eyes, and
    • your line of sight up to the top.

    It's always measured up from the horizontal, never from the wall or from straight up.

    This makes a right triangle: the horizontal line along the bottom, the building up the side, and your line of sight as the hypotenuse.

    You can measure it with a simple tool called a clinometer, or with a free angle-measuring app on a phone.

  3. 3
    Looking down

    The angle of depression

    When you look down from somewhere high, like the top of a cliff, the angle of depression is the angle between the flat line straight ahead and your line of sight down.

    Here's a useful fact: the angle of depression from the cliff is the same as the angle of elevation from the boat looking up.

    Why? The flat line at the top of the cliff and the sea are parallel. Your line of sight crosses both of them at the same slant, so it makes the same angle with each. Look for the Z shape in the picture.

    So you can always use the angle at the bottom, inside the right triangle.

  4. 4
    Don't forget your eyes

    The triangle starts at your eyes

    When you look up at a tree, your eyes aren't on the ground. They're about 1.5 m up.

    So the triangle you solve only gives the height above your eyes. To get the full height, add your eye height.

    Example

    You stand 20 m from a tree. The angle of elevation to the top is 40° (tan 40° ≈ 0.84). Your eyes are 1.5 m above the ground.

    Height above your eyes: 20 × 0.84 = 16.8 m. Full height: 16.8 + 1.5 = 18.3 m.

    This is the most common mistake in real measuring. If a problem says "your eyes are 1.5 m above the ground", that 1.5 m is there to be added.

  5. 5
    How to solve

    Draw it first

    1. Draw a picture. Put in the ground, the thing you're measuring, and where you are.
    2. Draw the flat line from the eyes, and the line of sight. Mark the right angle.
    3. Mark the angle and the side you know. Put a ? on the side you want.
    4. Solve the right triangle as before: pick sin, cos or tan, then multiply or divide.
    5. Finish: add any extra height (like your eyes), and check the answer makes sense.
    Example: how far is the boat?

    A lighthouse is 40 m tall. From the top, the angle of depression to a boat is 20° (tan 20° ≈ 0.364). How far is the boat from the bottom of the lighthouse?

    1. Draw it: lighthouse up the side, the sea along the bottom, the line of sight from the top down to the boat.
    2. The angle at the boat is also 20° (step 3). From there, the lighthouse is opposite and the distance is adjacent: TOA, so tan.
    3. tan 20° = 40 ÷ ?. The ? is on the bottom, so divide.
    4. ? = 40 ÷ 0.364 ≈ 109.9 m. The boat is about 110 m away.
  6. 6
    Two triangles

    A flagpole on a roof

    Sometimes one triangle isn't enough. Say there's a flagpole on top of a building, and you want the length of the pole.

    Stand 20 m away and measure two angles: up to the top of the building, and up to the top of the flag. That gives two triangles that share the same bottom side (the 20 m).

    • Building: angle 30°, so height = 20 × tan 30° ≈ 20 × 0.5774 ≈ 11.55 m
    • Top of flag: angle 40°, so height = 20 × tan 40° ≈ 20 × 0.8391 ≈ 16.78 m
    • The pole is the difference: 16.78 − 11.55 ≈ 5.2 m (keep the decimals until the end, like in Level 4)

    Big problems are often just two small triangles. Find each one, then add or subtract.

  7. 7
    Real life

    Who measures like this?

    • Surveyors

      They use a tool called a theodolite to measure angles very precisely, to map land and plan roads and buildings.

    • Sailors

      A lighthouse's height is on the sea map. One angle tells them how far they are from the rocks.

    • Pilots

      Planes land along a slope of about 3°. From the height and that angle, they know how far the runway is.

    • Ancient Greeks

      The story goes that Thales worked out the height of a pyramid, more than 2,500 years ago, by comparing its shadow with the shadow of a stick.

    Does it make sense?
    • Walk closer and the angle of elevation gets bigger. Walk away and it gets smaller.
    • A small angle of depression means far away. A boat near the cliff needs a steep angle.
    • At 45°, the height above your eyes equals the distance (tan 45° = 1). Below 45° it's shorter, above 45° it's taller.
    • Did you add the eye height?
