How much electricity flows? (Current)

Level 1 showed that charges flow round a closed loop. Now we measure how much flows each second. Go through the 7 short steps, then try the current meter and the practice.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    Which charger fills your phone faster?

    A charger marked 2 A fills a phone faster than one marked 1 A. "A" means amperes: how much electricity flows each second. That flow is called the current.

    Look at the small print on two phone chargers. One says 1 A. The other says 2 A.

    Plug the same empty phone into each one. The 2 A charger fills it much faster.

    What does the "A" mean? And why does a bigger number fill the phone faster? By the end of this level, you'll be able to work out exactly how much faster.

    Where else you'll see amperes

    On plugs, extension leads and circuit breakers, which say how much current they can safely carry. A small LED lamp uses well under 1 A. A car's starter motor needs a huge current, over 100 A, for a few seconds.

    Why it matters

    Too much current makes wires hot. Knowing the current tells you if a cable, a plug or a charger is safe. Level 4 explains why wires heat up.

  2. 2
    The big idea

    Current is how much charge passes each second

    Electric current (I) is the amount of electric charge that passes one point in a wire each second. Charge is measured in coulombs (C). Current is measured in amperes (A).

    In Level 1, charges flow round the loop like water in pipes. Now we ask: how much?

    Stand at one point of a water pipe with a stopwatch. Count the litres that pass you each second. A strong pump and a wide pipe: lots of water each second. A weak pump: only a little.

    Do the same at one point in a wire, but count electric charge. The amount of charge that passes each second is the current شدة التيار.

    Charge Q
    How much electricity. Measured in coulombs (C) كولوم.
    Time t
    Measured in seconds (s).
    Current I
    Charge passing each second. Measured in amperes (A) أمبير, or "amps" for short.

    Why count at just one point?

    In a single loop, the current is the same everywhere. Charges can't pile up anywhere, so whatever passes one point must also pass every other point. It's like cars on a one-lane roundabout: the same number pass every spot each minute.

    Why the letter I?

    It comes from "intensity". Your book calls it the electric current intensity: "the quantity of electric charge that passes through a cross-section of a conductor in one second."

    Who were Coulomb and Ampère?

    Two French scientists from the 1700s and 1800s. The units of charge and current are named after them.

  3. 3
    The rule

    Current = charge ÷ time

    I = Q ÷ t. One ampere means one coulomb passes each second. Always change the time to seconds first.
    I = Qtcurrent = charge ÷ time

    Divide the charge by the time, and you get how much passes in each second.

    So 1 ampere = 1 coulomb per second (1 A = 1 C/s). "The current is 0.2 A" means 0.2 coulombs pass each second.

    Example from your book

    A charge of 5400 C passes through a wire in half an hour. What is the current?

    1. Time in seconds: half an hour = 0.5 × 60 × 60 = 1800 s.
    2. I = Q ÷ t = 5400 ÷ 1800 = 3 A.

    Time must be in seconds. Minutes × 60. Hours × 3600 (60 × 60).

    Why seconds?

    An ampere is coulombs per second. If you divide by minutes, you get coulombs per minute, which is 60 times too big. In the example, 5400 ÷ 30 minutes = 180, which is far too big. The right answer is 3 A.

    How your book defines the units

    The ampere: "the current intensity resulting from the passage of an electric charge of one coulomb through a cross-section of a conductor in one second."

    The coulomb: "the quantity of electric charge transferred by a steady current of one ampere in one second."

  4. 4
    Turn it around

    Finding the charge or the time

    Cover the one you want: I = Q ÷ t, Q = I × t, t = Q ÷ I. If you want Q (on top), multiply. If you want I or t, divide.

    It's the same cover-up triangle from Trigonometry Level 2. Put Q on top, and I and t on the bottom. Cover the one you want (shaded below), and the other two tell you what to do: side by side means multiply, one above the other means divide.

    Find the charge

    A current of 6 A flows for 2 minutes. How much charge passes?

    1. Time in seconds: 2 × 60 = 120 s.
    2. Q = I × t = 6 × 120 = 720 C.
    Find the time

    40 C of charge passes at a current of 5 A. How long does it take?

    1. Want t, so divide: t = Q ÷ I.
    2. t = 40 ÷ 5 = 8 s.

    Check that the answer makes sense

    A bigger current for the same time should move more charge. A bigger current should move the same charge in less time. If your answer goes the other way, you've multiplied when you should divide, or the other way round.

    The units check themselves

    Amperes × seconds = coulombs (A × s = C). Coulombs ÷ amperes = seconds (C ÷ A = s). If the units come out right, the formula is probably right too.

  5. 5
    What happens if…?

    Change the charge or the time, and the current changes

    More charge in the same time → more current (direct relationship). The same charge over more time → less current (inverse relationship). Double the charge and halve the time → 4 times the current.

    Your book often asks "what happens to the current if…?". Write I = Q ÷ t and think: anything that grows on top makes I bigger. Anything that grows on the bottom makes I smaller.

    If…Example (start: 100 C in 10 s = 10 A)Current
    Charge doubles, same time200 ÷ 10 = 20 A× 2
    Time doubles, same charge100 ÷ 20 = 5 A÷ 2
    Charge doubles and time halves200 ÷ 5 = 40 A× 4
    Charge triples and time halves300 ÷ 5 = 60 A× 6

    Your book's words: at constant time, current and charge have a direct relationship (one goes up, the other goes up). At constant charge, current and time have an inverse relationship (one goes up, the other goes down).

    The graphs

    Current against charge (same time) is a straight line through zero: double one, double the other. Current against time (same charge) is a curve that falls: the longer it takes, the smaller the current.

    Watch the words

    "The charge increases by twice its value" means it goes up by 2 × Q, so it becomes 3 times as much (Q + 2Q). That's the last row of the table.

  6. 6
    Measuring it

    The ammeter goes in the loop (in series)

    An ammeter measures current in amperes. It's connected in series: inside the loop itself, so all the current flows through it. Its red (+) terminal goes towards the battery's +.

    An ammeter الأميتر measures current in amperes. In circuit diagrams it's a circle with an A inside.

    It's connected in series على التوالي. You open the loop and put the ammeter in the gap, so every charge has to pass through it. It's like a water meter fitted into the pipe itself.

    Connect its red (+) terminal to the side that leads to the battery's +, and its black (−) terminal to the side that leads to −. The other way round, the needle tries to move backwards.

    Why not just beside the circuit?

    If the ammeter isn't in the loop, the current doesn't go through it, so it can't count anything. A water meter lying next to the pipe measures nothing.

    Where in the loop?

    Anywhere. In a single loop the current is the same everywhere (step 2), so the ammeter reads the same before or after the bulb.

    Coming in Level 3

    The voltmeter measures the push. It's connected the other way: across a part, not in the loop.

  7. 7
    Back to the chargers

    Now we can solve step 1

    A 2 A charger moves 2 C every second, twice as much as a 1 A charger. To move the same charge, it takes half the time: t = Q ÷ I.
    • A 1 A charger moves 1 coulomb of charge into the phone every second. A 2 A charger moves 2 coulombs every second.
    • A typical phone battery holds about 14,400 C. With t = Q ÷ I:
    • 1 A: 14,400 ÷ 1 = 14,400 s = 4 hours. 2 A: 14,400 ÷ 2 = 7,200 s = 2 hours.

    Double the current, half the time. That's the inverse relationship from step 5.

    • Phone makers

      "Fast charging" just means a bigger current.

    • Electricians

      Choose cables thick enough for the current they'll carry.

    • Car mechanics

      Test that a battery can give the big current a starter motor needs.

    Where does 14,400 C come from?

    Phone batteries are labelled in mAh (milliamp-hours). A 4000 mAh battery can give 4000 milliamps (4 A) for 1 hour: 4 × 3600 = 14,400 C.

    Is a bigger current always better?

    No. Bigger currents make wires hotter. That's why fast chargers come with good cables, and why the phone controls the current near the end. Real charging also slows down as the battery gets full, so these times are a rough guide.
