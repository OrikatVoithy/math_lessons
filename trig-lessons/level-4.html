Special angles: 30°, 45° and 60°

So far a table or a calculator gave you sin, cos and tan. For three special angles, you can work them out yourself, from a square and a triangle. Go through the 7 short steps one at a time.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    Why learn this?

    The same three angles keep coming up

    Some angles turn up again and again, because they come from two very simple shapes:

    • 45° comes from a square cut corner to corner. Think of a picture frame: its corners are cut at 45°.
    • 60° and 30° come from an equilateral triangle (all three sides the same). Bees build honeycombs from them, and roofs and bridges are full of them.

    If you know sin, cos and tan for these three angles, you can:

    • solve problems without a calculator (some tests don't allow one);
    • check a calculator answer in your head;
    • estimate other angles, like 35° or 50°.

    And you won't need to memorise anything: you'll work them out from the shapes.

  2. 2
    45° from a square

    Cut a square corner to corner

    Take a square with sides of 1. Cut it from one corner to the opposite corner. You get two right triangles.

    The cut splits the square corner (90°) exactly in half, so the other two angles are 45° each.

    The two short sides are both 1. To find the long side (the hypotenuse), use Pythagoras:

    Pythagoras' rule

    In a right triangle: short side² + other short side² = hypotenuse²

    Here: 1² + 1² = 2, so the hypotenuse is √2, the number that times itself makes 2. √2 ≈ 1.41.

    Now use SOH CAH TOA from the 45° corner:

    sin 45° = 1 ÷ √2 ≈ 0.71 cos 45° = 1 ÷ √2 ≈ 0.71 tan 45° = 1 ÷ 1 = 1

    These are the same numbers as the 45° row of the Level 1 table. Now you know where they come from! (Some books write 1 ÷ √2 as √2 ÷ 2. It's the same number, about 0.71.)

  3. 3
    30° and 60° from a triangle

    Cut an equilateral triangle in half

    Take an equilateral triangle with all sides 2. All three angles are 60° (they add up to 180°).

    Cut it straight down the middle. You get two right triangles. In each half:

    • the bottom is cut in half, so it's 1;
    • the sloping side is still 2 (the hypotenuse);
    • the top angle is cut in half, so it's 30°. The bottom angle is still 60°.

    For the height, use Pythagoras again: 1² + height² = 2², so height² = 3 and the height is √3 ≈ 1.73.

    sin 60° = √3 ÷ 2 ≈ 0.87 cos 60° = 1 ÷ 2 = ½ tan 60° = √3 ÷ 1 ≈ 1.73 sin 30° = 1 ÷ 2 = ½ cos 30° = √3 ÷ 2 ≈ 0.87 tan 30° = 1 ÷ √3 ≈ 0.58
  4. 4
    Why it works

    Why sin 30° is the same as cos 60°

    30° and 60° are the two corners of the same triangle. The side opposite the 30° corner is the side next to the 60° corner. So sin 30° and cos 60° are the same division: 1 ÷ 2. (You saw this swap in Level 1.)

    Here are all the values together. Look for the pattern in the sin row: the numbers under the √ go 1, 2, 3, and you always divide by 2. The cos row is the same, backwards.

    30°45°60°

    cos√3 ÷ 2≈ 0.87√2 ÷ 2≈ 0.71√1 ÷ 2= ½

    The pattern helps you remember, but you never have to: if you forget, draw the square or the triangle and work it out again.

  5. 5
    Exact values

    Why write √3 ÷ 2 and not just 0.87?

    Because √3 ÷ 2 is exact, and 0.87 is rounded. Usually the difference is tiny, but it grows when things get big.

    Say a straight road climbs at 60° for 1000 m (a very steep road!). Its height is 1000 × sin 60°:

    • with 0.87: 1000 × 0.87 = 870 m
    • exact: 1000 × √3 ÷ 2 ≈ 866 m

    That's a 4 m mistake from rounding. Engineers keep exact values until the very last step, then round once.

    Knowing the exact values also lets you check your calculator: if it says sin 30 is −0.99, you know straight away that something is wrong (it should be ½), probably the DEG setting.

  6. 6
    Solve without a calculator

    Same steps, special values

    1. Name the sides from θ.
    2. Pick sin, cos or tan from the two sides (SOH CAH TOA).
    3. Use the special value instead of a calculator: ½, √2 ÷ 2, √3 ÷ 2, 1 or √3.
    4. Finish as before: multiply or divide to find a side, or go backwards to find an angle.
    Example: find a side

    An 8 m ladder leans on a wall at 30° to the ground. How high does it reach?

    1. The height is opposite the 30°. The ladder is the hypotenuse.
    2. Opposite and hypotenuse: SOH, so sin.
    3. sin 30° = ½
    4. height = 8 × ½ = 4 m. No calculator needed!
    Example: find an angle

    A 6 m ladder has its foot 3 m from the wall. What angle does it make with the ground? Is it safe?

    1. The 3 m is adjacent. The ladder is the hypotenuse.
    2. Adjacent and hypotenuse: CAH, so cos.
    3. cos θ = 3 ÷ 6 = ½
    4. Which angle has a cos of ½? cos 60° = ½, so θ = 60°. Not safe: a safe ladder is about 75°, so move the foot closer to the wall.
  7. 7
    Real life

    Where special angles live

    • Your geometry set

      The two triangle rulers (set squares) are exactly these triangles: one is 45°-45°-90°, the other is 30°-60°-90°.

    • Honeycombs and tiles

      A hexagon is six equilateral triangles, so all its angles come from 60°. Bees and floor tiles both use them.

    • Roofs and bridges

      Triangles keep their shape under heavy loads, so builders use equilateral and 30°-60°-90° triangles in roof frames and bridges.

    • Picture frames

      Carpenters cut the corners at 45°, so two pieces meet to make a perfect square corner.

    Estimate any angle

    The special angles are like signposts. sin 30° = 0.5 and sin 45° ≈ 0.71, so sin 40° must be somewhere in between, a bit nearer 0.71. (The calculator says 0.64.) Use them to check that an answer is sensible.
