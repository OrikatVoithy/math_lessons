How do push, flow and resistance fit together? (Ohm's law)

You know the flow (current), the push (potential difference) and what slows it (resistance). One simple rule joins all three. Go through the 7 short steps, then repeat Ohm's own experiment in the lab.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    Can you predict the current before switching on?

    A water heater's label says 240 V and 8 A. Our homes give 220 V. What current will it take here? Ohm's law answers it in two lines.

    A water heater from abroad has a label: 240 V, 8 A. That's the current it takes at 240 volts.

    But the sockets in Egypt give 220 V. Will it take more current, less, or the same? And exactly how much?

    Electricians answer questions like this every day, before they switch anything on. By the end of this level, you can too.

    Why predicting matters

    Too much current can overheat wires and blow fuses. Too little and a device won't work properly. Knowing the current in advance keeps people and equipment safe.

    Three quantities, one rule

    Current (Level 2), potential difference (Level 3) and resistance (Level 4) aren't separate facts. Change one and the others respond. Ohm found out exactly how.

  2. 2
    Ohm's experiment

    Double the push, double the flow

    Ohm connected a resistor, an ammeter (in series) and a voltmeter (across it). Each time he raised the voltage, the current rose by the same proportion. Voltage ÷ current always came out the same: 10.

    Here's the experiment from your book. A fixed resistor is connected to a battery, a switch and a rheostat, with an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the resistor. Moving the rheostat changes the voltage, and each pair of readings is recorded:

    Voltmeter (V)1020304050
    Ammeter (A)12345
    V ÷ I1010101010

    Every time the voltage goes up, the current goes up by the same proportion. And voltage ÷ current is always 10. That constant number is the resistor's resistance: 10 Ω.

    Why use a rheostat?

    To change the voltage across the resistor step by step, as in Level 4.

    Give reason: the rheostat is set to its maximum at the start of the experiment. To pass the minimum current through the circuit, to protect the measuring devices (the ammeter and voltmeter) from damage.

    The variables

    Independent (what you change): the potential difference across the resistor. Dependent (what you measure): the current through it. Controlled (kept the same): the fixed resistance, at a constant temperature.

    The graph

    Plotting current against voltage gives a straight line through zero. Your book calls this a direct relationship.

    Who was Ohm?

    Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854), a German physicist. He discovered this law, and the unit of resistance is named after him.

  3. 3
    The law

    Ohm's law: I = V ÷ R

    Ohm's law: at constant temperature, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it. V ÷ I = R, so I = V ÷ R. 1 ohm = 1 volt per ampere.
    Your book's statement

    Ohm's law قانون أوم: the electric current intensity passing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference between its terminals, at constant temperature.

    I = VRcurrent = push ÷ resistance

    It makes sense: a bigger push drives more current, and more resistance lets less through. Like water: a taller tank pushes more water through a pipe, and a narrower pipe lets less through.

    From V ÷ I = R: 1 ohm = 1 volt per ampere (1 Ω = 1 V/A). A 10 Ω resistor needs 10 V to push 1 A through it.

    Your book's definitions that come from Ohm's law

    Electrical resistance: "the ratio of the potential difference between the terminals of a conductor to the electric current intensity passing through it."

    The ohm: "the resistance of a conductor carrying a current of 1 A when the potential difference between its terminals is 1 V."

    The ampere: "the current passing through a resistance of 1 Ω when the potential difference between its terminals is 1 V." The volt: "the potential difference between the terminals of a resistance of 1 Ω when a current of 1 A passes through it."

    Why "at constant temperature"?

    Because resistance changes with temperature (Level 4). If the conductor heats up, its resistance rises and V ÷ I is no longer constant. A lamp filament, which gets very hot, doesn't follow Ohm's law well for exactly this reason.

    Same idea, three ways

    V ÷ I = R, I = V ÷ R and V = I × R are the same law written three ways. The next step shows how to switch between them.

  4. 4
    Turn it around

    Finding V, I or R

    Triangle with V on top: I = V ÷ R, V = I × R, R = V ÷ I. Want V (on top)? Multiply. Want I or R? Divide.

    Same cover-up triangle again, with V on top and I and R underneath.

    Find R (book)

    A heater coil carries 5 A when the potential difference across it is 220 V. What is its resistance?

    1. Want R: R = V ÷ I.
    2. R = 220 ÷ 5 = 44 Ω.
    Find V (book)

    A vacuum cleaner has a resistance of 22 Ω and carries 10 A. What is the potential difference across it?

    1. Want V, on top: multiply.
    2. V = I × R = 10 × 22 = 220 V.
    Find I (book)

    A metal wire of resistance 2 Ω has 6 V across it. What current flows?

    1. Want I: I = V ÷ R.
    2. I = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 A.

    Units check

    Volts ÷ ohms = amperes. Amperes × ohms = volts. Volts ÷ amperes = ohms. If the units come out right, the rule is right.

  5. 5
    Two relationships

    More push, more current. More resistance, less current.

    At constant resistance, current and voltage are directly related (straight-line graph). At constant voltage, current and resistance are inversely related (falling curve). Double R → half the current. Double V → double the current.
    If…Example (start: 12 V across 6 Ω = 2 A)Current
    Resistance doubles, same voltage12 ÷ 12 = 1 A÷ 2
    Voltage doubles, same resistance24 ÷ 6 = 4 A× 2
    Voltage doubles and resistance halves24 ÷ 3 = 8 A× 4
    Voltage and resistance both double24 ÷ 12 = 2 Asame

    Finding R from a graph (book)

    On a graph of current against voltage, pick two points and use R = (V₂ − V₁) ÷ (I₂ − I₁). Your book's example: the voltage goes from 10 V to 20 V and the current from 2 A to 4 A, so R = (20 − 10) ÷ (4 − 2) = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 Ω.

    If the resistor burns out

    In Ohm's experiment, if the fixed resistor burns out, the loop is broken: the ammeter reads zero, and the voltmeter, still connected across the gap to the battery, shows the battery's voltage.

    How your book says the consequences

    "Increasing the electrical resistance to double, while the temperature remains constant (with respect to the electric current): the electric current intensity decreases to its half."

    "Increasing the potential difference between the two terminals of a conductor to double, while the temperature remains constant: the electric current intensity increases to double."

    Which one is the "controlled" variable?

    In each relationship, one quantity is kept the same on purpose (the controlled variable). For the straight line, resistance is kept constant. For the curve, voltage is kept constant.

  6. 6
    Putting it all together

    One family of formulas and units

    Four quantities, three rules: I = Q ÷ t, V = W ÷ Q, I = V ÷ R. Each unit can be written using the others: Ω = V/A, A = C/s = V/Ω, V = J/C = A × Ω, C = A × s, J = V × C. Some book problems need two rules in a row.

    Everything from Levels 2 to 5 fits together. These are the units your book asks you to recognise:

    • ohm (Ω) = volt ÷ ampere
    • ampere (A) = coulomb ÷ second = volt ÷ ohm
    • volt (V) = joule ÷ coulomb = ampere × ohm
    • coulomb (C) = ampere × second = joule ÷ volt
    • joule (J) = volt × coulomb
    • second (s) = coulomb ÷ ampere
    • ohm (Ω) also = volt × second ÷ coulomb = joule ÷ (coulomb × ampere)
    Two steps (book)

    Find the charge that passes through a 1000 Ω resistor in 30 minutes, if the potential difference across it is 220 V.

    1. Ohm: I = V ÷ R = 220 ÷ 1000 = 0.22 A.
    2. Time: 30 × 60 = 1800 s.
    3. Q = I × t = 0.22 × 1800 = 396 C.
    Two steps (book)

    Find the work done to move 500 C through a 3 Ω conductor carrying 2 A.

    1. Ohm: V = I × R = 2 × 3 = 6 V.
    2. W = V × Q = 6 × 500 = 3000 J.

    A strategy for two-step problems

    Write down everything you're given. Find the quantity you want, then ask: which rule gives it? If that rule needs something you don't have, find that first with another rule.

    "What is measured by…?"

    Your book asks things like "Mention the quantity measured by V/A" (resistance) or "by J/C" (potential difference). Use the list above.

  7. 7
    Back to the heater

    Now we can solve step 1

    From the label: R = 240 ÷ 8 = 30 Ω. At 220 V: I = 220 ÷ 30 ≈ 7.33 A. Less push, same resistance, so a little less current.
    • The heater's resistance stays the same. From the label: R = V ÷ I = 240 ÷ 8 = 30 Ω.
    • At 220 V: I = V ÷ R = 220 ÷ 30 ≈ 7.33 A.
    • Less push through the same resistance, so a little less current. The heater still works, but heats a little more slowly.

    • Electricians

      Work out currents in advance to choose safe cables and fuses.

    • Electronics

      Pick a resistor so an LED gets just the current it needs.

    • Safety

      Wet skin has a lower resistance, so the same voltage pushes a bigger, more dangerous current.

    Why is the resistance the same at both voltages?

    Resistance depends on the heater's wire (its length, thickness and material; Level 4), not on the voltage. We're assuming its temperature, and so its resistance, stays about the same.

    Why it heats more slowly

    Less current at less voltage means less energy each second. Level 8 shows exactly how much less, with power.
