How hard are the charges pushed? (Potential difference)

Level 2 measured how much charge flows. Now we measure the push behind it: the energy each coulomb gets. Go through the 7 short steps, then try the voltmeter lab and the practice.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    Why can you hold a battery, but never touch a socket?

    Batteries, chargers and sockets are labelled in volts (V): 1.5 V, 5 V, 220 V. The volt measures how hard the charges are pushed: how much energy each coulomb gets. A socket's push is about 150 times a small battery's.

    Look at the labels on things that use electricity. Each one has a number of volts (V):

    • 1.5 Vtorch cell
    • 3.7 Vphone battery
    • 5 Vphone charger
    • 12 Vcar battery
    • 220 Vhome socket

    You can hold a 1.5 V cell in your hand all day. But touching the inside of a 220 V socket can kill. What is a volt, and why does a bigger number make it so much more dangerous?

    Volts and amperes are different things

    Amperes (Level 2) say how much charge flows each second. Volts say how hard each bit of charge is pushed. A lazy river can carry a lot of water slowly; a thin jet from a hose can push hard. Both matter, and they're measured separately.

    Who was Volta?

    Alessandro Volta, an Italian scientist, built the first battery in 1800 by stacking discs of two metals with salty cloth between them. The volt is named after him.

  2. 2
    The big idea

    Charges flow from a higher "electric level" to a lower one

    Every point in a circuit has an electric level, called its potential. Charges flow from the higher level to the lower one, like water from a higher tank to a lower one. The difference between two levels is the potential difference, or voltage.

    Remember the two water tanks from Level 1: water flows from the higher tank to the lower one, and stops when the levels are equal.

    Every point in a circuit has an "electric level" too. Scientists call it the electric potential. Current flows from the point with the higher potential (the battery's +) to the point with the lower potential (the −).

    The difference between the two levels is the potential difference فرق الجهد, also called the voltage. A bigger difference means a harder push, like water falling from a taller tank.

    Why "potential"?

    Water at the top of a tank has potential: it could fall and do work, like turning a wheel. Charge at a high electric level could also flow and do work, like lighting a bulb. "Potential" means "could do work".

    How your book says it

    "Electric charges flow through a conductor from the point of higher electric potential (the positive pole) to the point of lower electric potential (the negative pole), according to scientific convention." That's the same + to − rule from Level 1.

    Cause and effect: "The presence of a potential difference between the terminals of a conductor (cause) makes an electric current pass through it (effect)." The current depends on the potential difference, not on how much charge there is. A wire is full of charge, but with no difference in level, nothing flows.

    Give reason: "Current does not pass from a conductor at 20 V to one at 30 V." Because current flows from the higher potential to the lower one, so it goes from 30 V to 20 V.

  3. 3
    The rule

    Potential difference = work ÷ charge

    Potential difference is the work (energy) needed to move one coulomb between two points: V = W ÷ Q. Work is in joules (J). 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb.
    Your book's definition

    Potential difference between two points: the work done to move one coulomb of electric charge between those two points.

    V = WQpotential difference = work ÷ charge

    Think of each coulomb as a delivery bag. The battery fills every bag with energy, and the bulb empties it. The voltage tells you how much energy is in each bag.

    Work is measured in joules (J) جول, so 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb (1 V = 1 J/C). "The potential difference is 20 V" means 20 J of work is done to move each coulomb.

    Example from your book

    The work done to move 300 C of charge between two points is 33,300 J. What is the potential difference?

    1. Know: W = 33,300 J, Q = 300 C. Want: V.
    2. V = W ÷ Q = 33,300 ÷ 300 = 111 V.

    Work and energy are the same thing here

    "Work done" means energy used to do something. Moving a coulomb through the circuit takes energy, and the battery supplies it. Both are measured in joules.

    How your book defines the volt

    "The volt: the potential difference between two points when the work done to transfer a quantity of electric charge of one coulomb between them equals one joule."

    "What is meant by…?" with numbers

    "The work needed to move 8 C between the ends of a conductor is 64 J." It means V = W ÷ Q = 64 ÷ 8 = 8 V: the potential difference across the conductor is 8 V.

    What's a joule?

    About the energy needed to lift an apple (100 g) by 1 metre. A 1.5 V cell gives each coulomb 1.5 J, a bit more than one apple lift.

  4. 4
    Turn it around

    Finding the work or the charge

    Same cover-up triangle, with W on top: V = W ÷ Q, W = V × Q, Q = W ÷ V. If you want W (on top), multiply. If you want V or Q, divide.

    It's the same triangle as Level 2, with W on top and V and Q on the bottom. Cover the one you want (shaded).

    Find the work (book)

    3000 C of charge moves between two points with a potential difference of 3 V. How much work is done?

    1. Want W, on top: multiply.
    2. W = V × Q = 3 × 3000 = 9000 J.
    Find the charge (book)

    The potential difference is 15 V and the work done is 510 J. How much charge moved?

    1. Want Q: divide.
    2. Q = W ÷ V = 510 ÷ 15 = 34 C.

    Units check

    Volts × coulombs = joules (V × C = J). Joules ÷ volts = coulombs (J ÷ V = C). If the units come out right, you probably used the right rule.

    Sense check

    A bigger voltage moving the same charge needs more work. If your answer gets smaller when the voltage grows, you've divided when you should have multiplied.

  5. 5
    What happens if…?

    Change the work or the charge, and the voltage changes

    More work for the same charge → higher voltage (direct). The same work shared by more charge → lower voltage (inverse). Double the work and halve the charge → 4 times the voltage.

    Write V = W ÷ Q: anything that grows on top makes V bigger, and anything that grows on the bottom makes V smaller. Just like current in Level 2.

    If…Example (start: 100 J for 50 C = 2 V)Voltage
    Work halves, same charge50 ÷ 50 = 1 V÷ 2
    Charge halves, same work100 ÷ 25 = 4 V× 2
    Work doubles and charge halves200 ÷ 25 = 8 V× 4
    Work and charge both double200 ÷ 100 = 2 Vsame

    Your book's words: at constant charge, voltage and work have a direct relationship. At constant work, voltage and charge have an inverse relationship.

    Why does sharing work among more charge lower the voltage?

    Voltage is energy per coulomb. Share the same 100 J among twice as many coulombs, and each one gets half as much.

  6. 6
    Measuring it

    The voltmeter goes across (in parallel)

    A voltmeter measures potential difference in volts. It's connected in parallel: across the two points you're comparing, not inside the loop. Red (+) goes to the side nearer the battery's +.

    A voltmeter الفولتميتر measures the potential difference between two points, in volts. In diagrams it's a circle with a V inside.

    It compares two points, so it's connected across them, one wire to each side of the part you're measuring. This is called in parallel على التوازي. The loop isn't opened.

    Its red (+) terminal goes to the point nearer the battery's +, and its black (−) terminal to the point nearer the −.

    Why across, and not in the loop?

    To compare the levels of two tanks, you measure at both tanks. A voltmeter needs to touch both points at the same time. Put it inside the loop instead and it blocks most of the current, so the bulb goes dark.

    Across a battery

    Put the voltmeter across the battery's two ends and it shows the battery's voltage. That's how you test whether a cell is flat. Level 6 explains why the reading changes a little when the circuit is switched on.

  7. 7
    Back to the socket

    Now we can solve step 1

    A 1.5 V cell gives each coulomb 1.5 J. A 220 V socket gives each coulomb 220 J, about 150 times as much. That much energy per coulomb can push a dangerous current through your body.
    • A 1.5 V cell gives each coulomb 1.5 J of energy. That's too little to push much current through your skin.
    • A 220 V socket gives each coulomb 220 J: 220 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 147 times as much. It can push a dangerous current through your body.
    Putting Levels 2 and 3 together (book)

    The work done to move a charge is 20 J, it takes 2 s, and the potential difference is 5 V. What is the current?

    1. First the charge: Q = W ÷ V = 20 ÷ 5 = 4 C.
    2. Then the current from Level 2: I = Q ÷ t = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 A.

    • Electricians

      Use voltmeters to check whether a wire is "live" before touching it.

    • Drivers

      A car battery reading well below 12 V needs charging.

    • Power companies

      Send electricity across the country at very high voltages, then step it down to 220 V for homes.

    Is it the volts or the amps that hurt?

    What harms you is the current through your body. But your body resists current (Level 4), so only a high voltage can push a big current through it. That's why high voltage is the warning sign, and why wet hands are extra dangerous: water lets current through more easily.

    Birds on power lines

    A bird sits on one wire with both feet. Both feet are at the same electric level, so there's no potential difference across the bird, and no current flows through it.
