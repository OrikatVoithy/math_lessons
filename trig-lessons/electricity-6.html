How do you build a battery? (Cells and electromotive force)

One cell gives only 1.5 volts. Join several and you can get more push, or make it last longer, depending on how you join them. Go through the 7 short steps, then build batteries in the lab.

Step 1 of 7
  1. 1
    The problem

    A torch and a power bank both use several cells. Why join them differently?

    A torch joins its cells end to end, + to −, to get more push. A power bank joins its cells side by side, + to + and − to −, so it lasts longer. This level shows why.

    A single cell gives only about 1.5 V. That's not enough for many devices.

    Open a torch: the cells sit end to end, the + of one touching the − of the next. Open a power bank: its cells sit side by side, all the + ends joined together and all the − ends joined together.

    Same cells, joined two different ways. What does each way do, and how do you choose?

    Cells and batteries

    In everyday speech we call one cell a "battery", but in science a cell is one unit and a battery is two or more cells joined together. An electric car's battery is thousands of cells.

  2. 2
    The push of a source

    Electromotive force (emf): the total push of a cell

    Every cell or battery has an electromotive force (emf, symbol ε): the total work it does to move one coulomb round the whole circuit. It's measured in volts, with a voltmeter across the cell.
    Your book's definition

    Electromotive force (emf) القوة الدافعة الكهربية of a source: the total work done by the source to move one coulomb of electric charge through the whole electric circuit.

    Its symbol is ε, the Greek letter epsilon. Like potential difference, it's measured in volts, with a voltmeter connected across the cell's two ends.

    Cells and batteries are direct-current sources: they push current one way, at a steady strength.

    Emf vs potential difference

    Potential difference is the work per coulomb between two points, like across one bulb. Emf is the work per coulomb round the whole circuit: the source's entire push.

    Isn't it a "force"?

    No, the name is old and misleading. It's measured in volts, not in units of force. Just think of it as "the source's total push per coulomb".

    Your book's definitions of the two kinds of current

    Direct current: "an electric current that has a constant intensity and flows in one direction in the circuit." Alternating current: "an electric current whose value and direction change with time."

  3. 3
    Open and closed

    Why a battery reads a little less when it's working

    Circuit open: the voltage across the battery equals its emf. Circuit closed: current also flows inside the battery, which has a small internal resistance, so the voltage across its ends is a little less than the emf. In problems we ignore the internal resistance, so V = ε.
    • Circuit open (no current): the voltmeter across the battery shows its emf: V = ε.
    • Circuit closed (current flowing): current flows inside the battery too. Part of the emf is used up inside, so the voltage across its ends is a little less than the emf.

    Give reason: part of the emf is used inside the battery. To overcome the resistance opposing the current inside the battery (its internal resistance).

    Give reason: in problems, we take the internal resistance as negligible. Because it is very small compared with the external resistance. So in problems, the voltage across the battery = its emf.

    Water idea

    A pump has to push water through its own insides too. A little of its push is used up there, so slightly less is left for the pipes outside.

    Why a weak battery reads fine but won't work

    An old battery's internal resistance grows. With nothing connected, a voltmeter still reads nearly the full emf. Connect a device, and most of the emf is used up inside, so the device barely runs.

  4. 4
    Joining cells

    Two ways to join cells: series and parallel

    Series: unlike ends joined (+ to −), in a line. Parallel: like ends joined (all + together, all − together). In the book's activity with 1.5 V cells: two in series give 3 V and a bulb twice as bright; two in parallel give 1.5 V and the same brightness as one cell.
    Your book's definition

    Battery بطارية: two or more electric cells connected together in an electric circuit.

    In series على التوالي

    Unlike ends are joined: the + of one cell to the − of the next, and so on. The free − of the first cell and the free + of the last cell are the battery's two ends.

    In parallel على التوازي

    Like ends are joined: all the + ends together as one + pole, and all the − ends together as one − pole.

    The book's activity: three circuits, each with the same bulb and cells of 1.5 V.

    Why does series add up?

    Each cell gives every coulomb a push, one after another, like climbing two flights of stairs: you end up twice as high. In parallel, each coulomb goes through only one cell, so it gets one cell's push.

  5. 5
    The rules

    Series adds up. Parallel stays the same.

    Identical cells in series: ε (total) = ε (one cell) × n. In parallel: ε (total) = ε (one cell). More cells in series means more emf (direct); more cells in parallel leaves the emf the same.
    Series: εtotal = εcell × n
    Parallel: εtotal = εcell
    Book example

    A lamp is connected to three cells in series, each 3 V. What is the potential difference across the lamp?

    1. V across the lamp = ε of the battery (internal resistance negligible).
    2. ε = 3 × 3 = 9 V.
    Book example

    Four cells of 1.5 V are joined with all + ends together and all − ends together. Series or parallel? What is the emf?

    1. Like ends joined: parallel.
    2. ε = 1.5 V, the same as one cell.

    Comparing the two (book)

    Series: + of one to − of the next; emf = the sum of the cells' emfs; a lamp is brighter than with one cell. Parallel: + ends together and − ends together; emf = one cell's emf; a lamp is about as bright as with one cell.

  6. 6
    Mixed and designed

    Mixed batteries, and building one for a job

    Mixed battery: ε = ε (the parallel group, counts as one cell) + ε (the cells in series). To design: target = one cell → all in parallel; target = all cells added → all in series; in between → put cells in series until you reach the target, then join the rest in parallel with one of them.

    Mixed battery: εbattery = εparallel group + εremaining cells in series. A parallel group counts as just one cell.

    Book example

    Battery X has two cells in parallel, then two more in series with that group. Each cell is 1.5 V. What is its emf?

    1. Parallel group: 1.5 V (counts as one cell).
    2. Plus two cells in series: 1.5 + 1.5 × 2 = 4.5 V.

    Designing a battery (book guidelines):

    1. Target = one cell's emf → join all the cells in parallel.
    2. Target = all the cells added together → join all in series.
    3. Target in between → join cells in series until you reach the target, then join the remaining cells in parallel with one of the series cells.
    Book example

    Three 1.5 V cells. How do you get 1.5 V, 4.5 V and 3 V?

    1. 1.5 V (one cell's worth): all three in parallel.
    2. 4.5 V (all three added): all three in series.
    3. 3 V (in between): two in series = 3 V, then the third in parallel with one of them.

    Why put the spare cell in parallel instead of leaving it out?

    A cell in parallel adds no push, but it shares the work with its partner, so that pair runs down more slowly. Nothing is wasted.

    Two parallel groups

    Your book also shows two series groups of three 1.5 V cells, joined in parallel. Each group is 4.5 V, and joining equal groups in parallel keeps it at 4.5 V.

  7. 7
    Back to the torch

    Now we can solve step 1

    Torch: cells in series to get a bigger emf, enough to power it. Power bank: cells in parallel so the current lasts longer without raising the output voltage, so it can charge a phone several times.
    • Torch: give reason, its cells are in series. To get a greater electromotive force, enough to power it. Two 1.5 V cells in series give 3 V.
    • Power bank: give reason, its cells are in parallel. To keep the current going for a longer time without increasing its output voltage, so the phone can be charged several times.

    So: series for more push, parallel for more time.

    • TV remotes

      Two 1.5 V cells in series make 3 V.

    • Electric cars

      Thousands of cells, in series for high voltage and in parallel for long range.

    • Solar lamps

      Store energy in cells joined to suit the lamp.

    Never mix old and new cells

    In series, a weak cell drags the whole battery down. In parallel, a strong cell can push current backwards into a weak one, heating it up. Always use identical cells, as your book's rules assume.
